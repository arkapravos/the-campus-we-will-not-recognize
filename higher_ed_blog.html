The Campus We Will Not Recognize

American higher education is entering a decade of profound structural change. Here is what the research is telling us, what is already happening on the ground, and why the choices institutions make right now will determine who survives.

AS
Arka Sarkar, PhD
Assistant Professor of Marketing, Sawyer Business School, Suffolk University

Something significant is happening in American higher education, and it is not the kind of change that shows up all at once. It accumulates quietly, in enrollment spreadsheets and presidential memos, in curriculum committees and board meetings, until one day you look up and realize the institution you knew is no longer the one in front of you.

That moment is arriving. The next ten years in higher education will not resemble the previous twenty. The forces now converging on the sector are not cyclical pressures that patient institutions can simply wait out. They are structural, demographic, technological, and political in nature, and they are arriving simultaneously. Understanding them is not just an academic exercise. For those of us who teach, lead, and build programs inside these institutions, it is genuinely essential.

01 / The Demographic PressureThe Enrollment Cliff Is Not a Metaphor

The data here is unambiguous. The number of high school graduates in the United States likely peaked around 2025 and will now begin a sustained decline. By 2035, the pipeline feeding into colleges will be roughly 10 percent smaller than it is today, and the effects will not be evenly distributed. The Northeast and the Midwest, home to an unusually dense concentration of colleges and universities, will absorb the steepest drops.

EAB, one of the leading research firms in higher education strategy, projects that by 2030, 449 colleges will face at least a 25 percent decline in their first-year enrollment. 182 of those will face a decline exceeding 50 percent. These are not hypothetical scenarios. These are institutions that, absent a dramatic strategic intervention, face closure or forced merger within the decade.

For context: undergraduate enrollment in the United States hit its peak of 18.1 million students in 2010-11. It is now closer to 15 million. The next contraction, driven by demographics, is already baked into the birth records of students who are currently in middle school.

"Business as usual is not an option. The next decade will require different approaches and different mindsets from the ones that built the expansionist higher education we have known."

02 / ConsolidationMergers, Acquisitions, and a Smaller Map

The response to this pressure is already visible in the headlines if you know where to look. Colleges and universities across the country are exploring mergers, acquisitions, and the creation of shared service networks. Indiana passed a state law requiring all public universities to offer at least one three-year degree program. Ohio State cut eight majors and merged twenty others to align with workforce demands. Pomona College entered acquisition negotiations with Claremont Graduate University. Elon University's president spoke openly about merger discussions.

The honest picture Deloitte's 2026 Higher Education report paints is this: the future likely holds fewer but stronger US colleges, as institutions that cannot sustain themselves independently choose to merge or form strategic partnerships rather than manage a slow decline alone.

This is not failure. This is rationalization, and in many cases it will be the right outcome. The more interesting strategic question is not whether consolidation will happen, but which institutions will be the acquirers, which will be the acquired, and which will find a way to thrive independently by being genuinely distinctive.

03 / The ROI ReckoningStudents Are Asking a Different Question

For most of the postwar era, the dominant question students asked when considering college was some version of "which college should I attend?" The question being asked today is increasingly "should I attend college at all?"

Deloitte's data makes the shift vivid. Only 22 percent of Americans believe a four-year degree is worth the cost when student loans are involved. In contrast, 76 percent of trade school graduates believe their skills-based education was worth the cost. Apprenticeships in the United States have more than doubled in the last decade, rising from 317,000 to 640,000 participants. These are not fringe numbers. They represent a genuine shift in how a generation is evaluating the return on their educational investment.

At the same time, 73 percent of prospective students cite affordability as a primary concern. This is not a passing anxiety. It is a structural feature of a generation that watched their older siblings emerge from college carrying debts that shaped their life choices for a decade.

04 / Credentials & PathwaysThe Four-Year Degree Loses Its Monopoly

The policy response to the ROI crisis is already underway, and it is moving faster than most faculty and administrators realize. The arrival of Workforce Pell in 2026, which extends federal financial aid to short-term credentials for the first time, is a genuinely significant structural change. For the first time, the financial architecture of American higher education is being deliberately redesigned to support pathways that are not built around the four-year degree.

More than 36 million Americans have attended college without completing a degree. This "some college, no credential" population grew by nearly 3 percent last year, and re-enrollment among this group rose by more than 9 percent in a recent academic year. Institutions that build serious, well-designed programs for adult learners returning to complete credentials are sitting on a substantial and underserved market.

The University of Texas System is already embedding industry-recognized microcredentials into bachelor's degree programs. Utah approved a new category of three-year degrees specifically designed to align with industry needs. The institutions that treat credential flexibility as a strategic priority, rather than a concession to market pressure, will be better positioned for what comes next.

05 / AI in Higher EducationFrom Classroom Tool to Institutional Infrastructure

Of all the forces reshaping higher education, artificial intelligence is the one that carries the most transformative potential and the most institutional uncertainty. University partnerships with AI companies expanded significantly in 2025, and the conversation has shifted from "what do we do about academic integrity?" to something considerably more ambitious.

The framing that I find most useful comes from a recent analysis: we have passed the moment where AI is a tool that higher education needs to manage. It has become institutional infrastructure, in the same category as the learning management system or the student information system, except that it is evolving orders of magnitude faster and reshaping what education itself means in the process.

"Institutions that operationalize AI will widen their performance gap, while those that do not will inherit a shadow system they cannot control."

What does this look like in practice? Agentic AI systems are already being deployed as Socratic tutors that scaffold difficult concepts in real time, as predictive intervention tools that identify students struggling in gateway courses before the first midterm, and as 24/7 advising supports that route students toward human counselors for complex situations. The Global AI in Education Market was valued at $3.6 billion recently and is projected to reach $73.7 billion by 2033.

For faculty and curriculum designers, the deeper shift is in assessment. The take-home essay as the primary assessment instrument is, practically speaking, finished. The question institutions are now grappling with is not how to prevent students from using AI, but how to design assessments that are genuinely meaningful in a world where AI is available. That means assessing process, judgment, and synthesis rather than product alone. It means designing for AI fluency as a graduation standard, not as an add-on.

As someone who has been building AI-integrated curricula at Suffolk, I will say plainly that this work is hard, it is genuinely exciting, and the institutions that invest in getting it right will have a durable competitive advantage over those that treat AI as a compliance problem to be managed.

06 / Accountability & OutcomesThe Performance Era Arrives for Everyone

New federal accountability frameworks are extending to all institutions the same kind of outcomes scrutiny that has historically applied mainly to for-profit colleges. Program-level data on cohort default rates, first-year earnings, Pell-recipient outcomes, and job placement rates are becoming universal compliance and reputational benchmarks. More than 1,700 programs across the country would fail or be at risk under the Department of Education's current accountability framework.

This is consequential for curriculum strategy. Programs that cannot demonstrate measurable workforce outcomes face real consequences going forward, not just reputational pressure. For humanities and social science programs in particular, this creates an urgent design challenge: how do you build curricula that preserve the genuine intellectual value of liberal education while also connecting students to clear, demonstrable pathways to economic participation?

The answer is not to abandon liberal education. It is to redesign it. The data from Tyton Partners shows rising student preference for interdisciplinary pathways that blend liberal arts with technical or career-aligned skills, while standalone majors in English, history, and communications continue to lose ground. The institutions that figure out how to embed applied competencies and AI fluency into their humanities offerings will be the ones that make those programs viable for the next generation.

07 / The Landscape in 2035Three Tiers for a Reshaped Sector

Putting all of this together, here is how I see the landscape sorting out by 2035. The sector will be meaningfully smaller, more differentiated, and considerably more accountable than the one we know today.

Tier One: Thriving

Research universities with large endowments, strong brand recognition, and the resources to invest in AI infrastructure, global partnerships, and flexible credential pathways. Elite institutions will be stronger, not weaker, by 2035.

Tier Two: Transforming

Mid-size public and innovative private institutions that move quickly to redesign curricula around career outcomes, invest in adult learner pathways, and build meaningful employer partnerships. These institutions can absolutely thrive with the right choices made now.

Tier Three: At Risk

Small, tuition-dependent institutions without a distinctive mission, strong placement outcomes, or clear geographic advantage. These are the colleges facing genuine existential questions by the mid-2030s.

The determining variable in which tier an institution occupies is not primarily endowment size or historical prestige. It is the quality, speed, and boldness of the strategic choices being made right now. Institutions that recognize the environment they are actually in, and design accordingly, have a real path forward. Those that wait for stability are going to find that it does not come.

08 / What This Means for UsA Final Word to Colleagues

I want to close with something direct, because I think the stakes here are worth naming plainly. Suffolk University, and the Sawyer Business School specifically, is not a passive observer of these trends. We are inside them. The choices we make about curriculum design, AI adoption, credential flexibility, employer engagement, and student outcomes are not administrative decisions. They are strategic ones, and their consequences will compound over the next decade.

The good news is genuine. We have assets that many institutions would trade for: an engaged urban student body, a location in one of the world's great knowledge economies, a faculty that has already demonstrated a willingness to innovate, and the kind of institutional scale that allows for meaningful change without the bureaucratic inertia of a very large system. The SAIL initiative, the AI-integrated curricula being built across departments, the shift toward outcome-focused program design: these are exactly the kinds of moves that position an institution well for what is coming.

The challenge is maintaining that momentum and being honest with ourselves when existing programs and practices are no longer serving our students as well as they should. The institutions that thrive in 2035 will be the ones that treated the current period of disruption not as a threat to be managed, but as a rare opening to build something genuinely better.

That is the opportunity in front of us. I, for one, am glad we are in it together.
